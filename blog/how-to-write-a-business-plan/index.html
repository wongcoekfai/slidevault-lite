How to Write a Business Plan: The Sections That Matter

2026-10-293 min read

Most business plans fail before the second page because they are written to impress rather than to decide. A useful plan is a thinking document: it forces you to answer the questions a lender, investor, or co-founder will ask, in the order they will ask them. Seven sections do the job.

1. Executive summary — write it last

One page, containing: what the business does, for whom, how it makes money, the traction so far, and what you need (money, people, a decision). If you cannot state the whole business in this space, the problem is upstream — the plan is not finished thinking.

2. Problem and market — prove it is real and reachable

Two questions, answered with evidence:

  • Is the problem frequent and painful enough to pay for? Use the buyer's language, not industry jargon.
  • Can you reach the customers? Size the market bottom-up: number of realistic target customers × achievable price. "$2B market" impresses no one who has seen a hundred plans; "4,000 dental clinics in our launch region, average software spend $3,600/year" reads like homework done.

3. Competitive landscape — show you know who else wants this

Name the alternatives, including the status quo (spreadsheets, manual work, doing nothing — the competitor everyone forgets). A simple positioning table with two axes that buyers actually care about beats a feature checklist. The conclusion to earn: a specific reason you win that is hard to copy.

4. Product and stage — what exists today

What is built, what works, and what the next two milestones are. Evidence over narrative: a screenshot, a pilot customer, waitlist numbers, letters of intent. Plans are judged on the distance between claims and proof — keep that distance short.

5. Go-to-market — the most neglected section

How customers hear about you, try you, and pay you — with the cost attached. One acquisition channel done deliberately beats six sketched vaguely. State the funnel: reach → trial → conversion, and the number that makes the model work. If customer acquisition cost is unknown, say so and name the experiment that will find it; honesty here reads as competence.

6. Financials — three years, defensible

  • Revenue built from the funnel in section 5, not from "capturing 1% of the market"
  • Costs split into fixed and per-unit, with the hiring plan tied to milestones
  • Break-even and the cash required to reach it
  • The assumptions list — every model is wrong somewhere; naming your assumptions is what separates a plan from a wish

7. The ask — specific and tied to milestones

What you need, what it buys, and what happens if you get it. "Raising $400k to reach 40 paying customers and $35k MRR in 18 months" gives the reader a decision to make. "Seeking investment to accelerate growth" gives them nothing.

One final test

Hand the plan to someone who knows the industry but not your company. If, without your commentary, they can tell you what you sell, who pays, why you win, and what you need — the plan works. If they cannot, the gap they hit is the section to rewrite first.
