How to Structure a Quarterly Business Review (QBR Deck)

2026-11-022 min read

Most QBRs fail in a predictable way: forty minutes of slideware recapping last quarter, ten minutes of arguing about a definition, and the meeting ends with no decision. A good quarterly business review is the opposite — a fixed agenda, numbers before stories, and every section built to produce one of three outputs: a decision, a resource change, or a commitment. Here is the structure.

The five-part agenda

  1. Scorecard: targets vs actuals — one slide, the five metrics the team actually committed to last quarter. Green, amber, red against each. No narrative yet; let the numbers land first.
  2. Where the numbers came from — the two or three drivers behind the biggest variances. If revenue missed by 12%, is it pipeline volume, win rate, or deal size? One slide per driver, cause before excuse.
  3. Pipeline truth for next quarter — coverage ratio by stage, deals at risk named individually, and the forecast with its confidence band. This is the section leadership actually attends for.
  4. Wins worth copying — two deals or projects, dissected: what the team did, and whether it can be repeated. A win without a mechanism is luck; a win with a mechanism is a play.
  5. Decisions and asks — headcount, budget reallocation, process changes. Each with an owner and a date. If this slide is empty, the meeting had no reason to exist.

The sales performance template follows this arc — targets, pipeline, wins, forecast — so the deck assembles in an hour instead of a weekend.

Separate reporting from deciding

The biggest structural mistake is mixing the two. Reporting (what happened) belongs in a pre-read sent 24 hours before; deciding (what we do) is the meeting. Teams that adopt the pre-read routinely cut their QBR from three hours to ninety minutes, because the room stops being a broadcast.

Kill the metric debate before the meeting

Nothing burns a QBR faster than ten minutes spent arguing whether a deal counts as "closed." Publish the metric definitions with the pre-read — what counts as pipeline, whose forecast rolls up where, what window the numbers cover — and disagreements surface while there is still time to fix the data.

Make the forecast a commitment, not a wish

A forecast without a confidence statement gets ignored. Better format: "Base case $1.9M at 70% confidence; commit $1.7M; upside $2.3M contingent on the two enterprise deals named below." Naming the specific deals that swing the number is what separates a forecast from a spreadsheet export.

Close with owners, not applause

End the meeting by reading back every decision with its owner and date. If nothing is written down, the same discussion repeats next quarter — which is the real cost of a bad QBR: not the meeting itself, but the quarter it fails to steer.
