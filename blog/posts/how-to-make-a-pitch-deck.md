Investors do not read pitch decks. They skim them — usually standing, often on a phone, frequently while answering email. Your deck has about ninety seconds of attention, and the decision to keep reading is made on the first three slides. Everything in this guide follows from that constraint.

## The ten slides, in the order investors expect

1. **Title** — company, one-line what-you-do, contact. Nothing else.
2. **Problem** — a specific, expensive, frequent pain. If the problem is not obvious in one sentence, the deck is over.
3. **Solution** — how you remove that pain, shown not explained. One screenshot or one diagram beats three paragraphs.
4. **Why now** — the market, technology, or regulation shift that makes this possible today and not three years ago. This is the slide most first-time founders skip and investors most want.
5. **Market** — bottom-up: who pays, how much, how many of them. "1% of a $50B market" is an instant red flag; "8,000 clinics × $4k/year" is credible.
6. **Traction** — revenue, users, retention, pilots, LOIs — whatever you have, trending. An honest flat line with a clear story beats an inflated hockey stick.
7. **Business model** — who pays, how much, how often, and the current unit economics if known.
8. **Competition** — a two-axis positioning map where you occupy an empty corner, plus the honest answer to "why won't the incumbent do this?"
9. **Team** — why these specific people win this specific market. Previous exits and domain scars, not job titles.
10. **The ask** — how much you are raising, what it buys (18–24 months of milestones), and what those milestones unlock.

## The first-three-slides test

Hand your deck to a friend who knows nothing about the space. After slides one to three — about twenty seconds — they should be able to answer: what does this company do, for whom, and why does it matter now? If they can't, nothing on slides four through ten will save the meeting. Iterate on the first three until this passes, then stop touching them.

## Cut these slides entirely

- **"We only need 1% of the market."** It signals you cannot compute a real market size.
- **The 18-slide feature tour.** Investors buy the outcome, not the settings menu.
- **NDA requests on slide one.** Universal instant credibility loss — ideas are cheap, execution is the moat.
- **Financial projections five years out.** Everyone knows they are fiction; a one-page unit economics slide is more convincing.

## How much text belongs on a slide

Roughly twenty words. If a slide needs a paragraph, it is two slides — one the presenter explains, one the skimmer reads. Numbers and visuals carry decks; sentences bury them. A practical drill: read only your slide titles in order. They should form a complete story by themselves — many investors genuinely do this on the first pass.

## Deck versus demo versus memo

For pre-seed and seed, the deck gets the meeting but the demo closes it — investors fund working products over beautiful slides at this stage. Later rounds flip: growth metrics dominate, and some funds prefer a three-page memo they can forward to partners. Build the deck first, but keep a two-paragraph email version ready; the forwardable summary often travels further than the file.
