The honest answer to "Google Slides or PowerPoint?" is that they have stopped competing on the same axis. Slides is a collaboration tool that presents; PowerPoint is a design tool that now collaborates. Which one you should open depends less on preference than on three questions: who edits, where they are, and what happens after the meeting. Here is the breakdown by what actually differs in 2026.

## Where Google Slides wins

- **Simultaneous editing.** Six people in one deck, live cursors, version history down to the keystroke. PowerPoint's co-authoring has improved, but the browser-native flow in Slides is still frictionless — no check-out, no sync state, no "someone has it open" errors.
- **Zero distribution cost.** A link replaces the file. No attachment-size limits, no "version 7_FINAL_v2.pptx" in inboxes, and reviewers always see the latest revision by default.
- **Commenting as a workflow.** Threads, @mentions, and assigned action items make review cycles measurably faster. For a deck that needs sign-off from four stakeholders, this alone decides it.
- **Price.** Included with a free Google account or any Workspace plan. PowerPoint requires a Microsoft 365 subscription for full features.

## Where PowerPoint is still ahead

- **Design depth.** Morph transitions, 3D models, advanced chart formatting, precise kerning and object alignment — the high-end polish features are still PowerPoint-only. If the deck is a product (a template you sell, a brand showcase), PowerPoint is the tool.
- **Offline reliability.** Slides works offline in the browser, but the desktop PowerPoint app remains the safer bet on planes, in hotel ballrooms, and on venue hardware you cannot control.
- **Ecosystem.** Add-ins, data-linked charts that refresh from Excel, and enterprise PowerPoint automation have no real Slides equivalent. Finance and consulting decks live here for a reason.
- **Fidelity under pressure.** Presenting a .pptx from a USB stick to an unknown projector still behaves more predictably than logging into a Google account on someone else's machine.

## The file-format trap (and how to avoid it)

Slides exports to .pptx, and PowerPoint imports Slides files — but each conversion can shift fonts, break animations, and reflow text boxes. Two rules prevent most pain:

1. **Decide the master format before editing starts**, not after. Converting a 40-slide deck at the last minute is where layouts die.
2. **Export early to test.** If the deck will end its life as a .pptx, do one conversion on slide one — fonts and spacing will tell you immediately whether the template is conversion-safe.

Well-built templates survive the round trip; the [professional business presentation template](https://slidevaulte.com/templates/professional-business-presentation-template/) renders cleanly in both tools, which is exactly what to look for if your audience is mixed.

## A practical decision rule

- **Team drafts, stakeholders comment, distributed by link** → Google Slides.
- **High-design deliverable, offline venue, data-heavy charts** → PowerPoint.
- **Mixed reality (most teams)** → draft in Slides for speed, finalize and present from PowerPoint, and keep the file in one format from the halfway point onward.
