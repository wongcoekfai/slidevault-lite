Most losing proposals are not losing on price. They lose because the client read three pages and still could not answer the only question that matters: what exactly do we get, and what happens after we sign? A winning proposal is short, ordered the way buyers read, and impossible to misunderstand. Here is the structure.

## Start with their problem, not your company

The first slide after the title should prove you listened. Restate the client's situation in their own words, with their numbers:

- "Onboarding takes 11 days and costs you roughly $40k per month in idle licenses"
- "Your proposal win rate is 18% while your top competitor sits at 31%"

If the client reads this section and thinks "yes, exactly" — you have already beaten half the field. Generic openers ("We are pleased to submit...") signal a template mail-merge and get skimmed.

## One solution slide per objective

Resist the urge to dump your full capability deck. For each objective the client named, one slide: what you will do, how it works, and the outcome it produces. Three objectives, three slides. Everything else — your process, your team, your technology — belongs in an appendix the reader visits only if they want to.

## Proof beats promises

After the solution, two kinds of evidence work:

1. **A relevant case study** — same industry, same problem, with a number attached. "Cut onboarding from 11 days to 3 for a 400-seat SaaS company" outperforms any adjective.
2. **A mini pilot or guarantee** — a small first step that lowers the risk of saying yes. Proposals with a low-risk entry point convert noticeably better than all-or-nothing ones.

## Price on options, not a single number

A single price forces a yes/no decision. Three options — good, better, best — turn the conversation into "which one". Rules that keep it clean:

- Anchor with a mid option; most buyers take it
- Make the differences concrete (deliverables, speed, support level), never "more value"
- Name what is excluded; surprises after signing kill renewals

## End with next steps so obvious they are boring

The last slide should state: what happens after they say yes, who does what, by when. "Approve by Friday → kickoff Monday → first results in two weeks." Proposals that leave the close implicit get filed under "we'll get back to you".

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Reusing the previous client's name in the file.** Check the first slide, the footer, and the file name.
- **Twelve pages of "about us".** The client hired a vendor, not a biography.
- **Pricing without dates.** Every quote should carry a validity window; it protects both sides.
- **No single owner on the client side.** Name the person you expect to sign, and address them.
