How Many Slides and Words Should a Presentation Have? (And How to Count Yours)

2026-11-095 min read

"How many slides for a 10-minute talk?" gets two answers. The lazy one is one slide per minute, so ten. The honest one is whatever you can say in ten minutes, which is usually fewer than you think.

The ratio still matters, because it is the fastest way to catch a deck that is going to run long — before you are standing in front of the room.

The ratios that hold up

Slot Slides Words on slides (total)
5 min 5–7 150–250
10 min 10–14 300–500
20 min 18–25 600–900
30 min 25–35 900–1,300
45–60 min 30–45 1,300–2,000

Two numbers underpin the table. Speaking pace lands around 130–150 words per minute — so a 10-minute slot carries roughly 1,300–1,500 spoken words. And slides should hold about a third of what you say, not all of it. That is the whole reason the table's word counts look low: the words on the slide are a cue, the words in the room are the content.

A demo, a workshop or a lecture with dense reference material breaks the pattern — a workshop can run one slide per three minutes and a statistics lecture one per ninety seconds. Use the table as a smell test, not a rule.

The 6×6 rule and where it fails

Six bullets, six words each, is the oldest guideline in presentation design and about as precise as it sounds. It is a decent default for a sales or marketing deck: it keeps a slide readable from the back of the room and forces you to speak the argument rather than read it.

It fails for financial reviews, academic work and legal material, where the numbers are the message and the slide is a reference document people photograph. In those decks, put the density into a table or a chart, keep the title a sentence, and accept that some slides are read rather than presented.

Where the numbers stop being advice

Sooner or later a hard limit decides for you:

  • Conference CFPs often cap decks at 15–20 slides, or cap the file size outright.
  • Learning platforms and internal portals reject uploads above 20–50 MB.
  • Email — most servers reject attachments over 20–25 MB, which is a size problem wearing a slide-count costume.
  • Brand and accessibility guides set maximum words per slide, and readable type limits words anyway: a 40-word slide at 24pt simply does not fit.
  • Timed slots — a 7-minute lightning talk with 20 slides is not a talk, it is a slideshow someone else narrates.

How to count what you actually have

Slides. The status bar in Normal view reads "Slide 3 of 24". View → Slide Sorter shows the count at the bottom of the window. Hidden slides still count as slides, so check for them if the number surprises you.

Words. PowerPoint has no word-count command. The usual workaround is to select everything on each slide and paste into Word — which drags in table cells, duplicates footers, and takes ten minutes on a 40-slide deck. It also merges notes with slide text, so the number you get is not the number you wanted.

What is heavy. File size tells you a deck is large; it never tells you why. A 5-image deck can weigh more than a 40-slide text deck, because a single 12-megapixel photo outweighs every word in the file several times over.

Reading the numbers

  • Words per slide over 60 on a presented (not read) deck: either split the slide or accept that the audience is reading and you are redundant.
  • Note words far above slide words: you have a script, which is good for rehearsal and a liability when the file goes out — strip the notes with Remove Notes first.
  • Media weight over 70% and a file over 20 MB: the deck will bounce out of inboxes. Compress PPT targets exactly that.
  • Slides well above the table for your slot: rehearse with a timer before you edit. Cutting content is easier after you have watched yourself run over.

Rehearsal math

A workable estimate: total words ÷ 140, plus 25 seconds per visual-heavy slide. A 900-word, 20-slide deck lands near 11 minutes of speaking — 6.5 minutes of words and four and a half minutes of pauses, transitions and the bit where someone asks a question.

If the result is 30% over your slot, trust it. The clock in the room is less forgiving than the estimate, and the two slides you cut in advance are always cheaper than the three you skip live.
