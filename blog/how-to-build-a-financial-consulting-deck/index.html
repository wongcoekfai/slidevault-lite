How to Build a Financial Consulting Deck an Investment Committee Will Actually Read

2026-11-124 min read

A consulting deck is usually read before it is presented. Someone opens it on a laptop twenty minutes before the meeting, scrolls to the valuation, and forms an opinion. By the time you reach slide four, the committee has already decided which assumption they want to challenge.

So build for the reader first. Put the recommendation on slide two, make every number auditable on the slide it appears, and keep the full model in the appendix rather than in the narrative.

The structure, in the order a committee reads it

  1. Recommendation (slide 2). One decision, one number, one condition. "Proceed at a £42–48m valuation range, subject to confirmatory diligence on churn." Everything after this slide exists to justify or qualify that sentence.
  2. Mandate and situation. What question was asked, by whom, and the time horizon. Two lines, not a company history.
  3. Market context. Only the three facts that change the recommendation: size and growth of the addressable segment, the competitive shift, and the constraint. Every market figure gets a source and a date on the same slide.
  4. The model in one view. A summary of the valuation or business case: key drivers, base case, and the range. Show the logic, not the spreadsheet.
  5. Scenarios and sensitivities. Base, downside, upside — and, more importantly, the two variables that move the answer most. A sensitivity table or tornado chart answers the question the committee will ask anyway.
  6. Risks and mitigations. Paired, with an owner or a trigger where possible. "Integration risk" alone is noise; "integration risk — 40% of synergies dependent on a single system migration by Q3" is a risk you can manage.
  7. Recommendation restated with next steps. Decision, owner, date, and what would change the recommendation.
  8. Appendix. Data sources, methodology, full model output, definitions, glossary, and the detailed diligence list.

Every number needs a source line

The fastest way to lose a committee is an unsourced market size. Add a small footer to each quantitative slide: source name, as-of date, unit, and appendix reference. Also state the currency and the basis (actual, forecast, annualised, pro forma) in the axis or column header, not only in the speaker notes.

Be consistent about units across the deck. Mixing millions and thousands between slides, or switching between calendar and fiscal years, produces the kind of ten-minute detour that kills a decision meeting. Before sending, use the PPT Stats tool to inventory the deck — it is a quick way to spot that the appendix has quietly grown to twice the length of the main body.

Do not paste spreadsheet screenshots

A screenshot of an Excel range looks like evidence but behaves like a dead image: nobody can read the assumptions, the numbers cannot be checked, and it scales badly on a projector. Rebuild the few rows that matter as a small native table, or use a single clean chart, and put the full model in the appendix as a linked table.

The same rule applies to charts with two different y-axes. If you need to show revenue growth and margin together, use two stacked charts sharing the same x-axis. Dual axes let you make any two lines look correlated.

Make the assumptions defensible, not invisible

Committees rarely object to the conclusion; they object to an assumption they cannot see. Put the three most consequential assumptions on the model slide itself, each with a range rather than a single point. When the downside case is shown, show what specifically breaks — not just a lower number.

Housekeeping before distribution

Client-facing financial decks carry a paper trail you do not want to share: prior versions, internal reviewer names, and edit history in the document properties. Clear them with Clean Metadata and strip draft commentary from the notes pane with Remove Notes. Both run locally in your browser. Then export the circulated copy to PDF so a font substitution on the client's machine cannot reflow a table during the meeting.
