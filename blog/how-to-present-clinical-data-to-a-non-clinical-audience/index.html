How to Present Clinical Data to a Non-Clinical Audience

2026-11-124 min read

A clinical results deck usually has to serve two readers at once: the specialist who wants the endpoint definition, confidence interval and subgroup analysis, and the administrator, board member or patient representative who needs to know what the finding means. Most decks pick one. The specialist version becomes unreadable; the plain-language version loses the evidence and gets dismissed.

The fix is not to simplify the numbers. It is to give every slide two layers: a plain-language headline that states the finding, and a compact evidence block underneath that a specialist can audit.

Write headlines as conclusions, not labels

"Efficacy Results" tells the audience nothing. "Primary endpoint met: 14.2% absolute reduction vs control" tells them the finding, and gives the specialist something to check. Put the population and time point in a smaller line beneath the headline so the claim is never floating free of its context.

Every slide should still be answerable in one sentence: what was measured, in whom, and what changed. If a slide needs a paragraph of narration to make sense, split it.

Keep the statistics visible but out of the way

Under the headline, use a consistent three-part evidence block:

  • The number with its denominator — "38/264 (14.4%)" rather than "14%".
  • The uncertainty — confidence interval, and the p-value only if your audience expects it. Never show a point estimate without the interval.
  • The source line — study name or data cut, date, and the appendix page with the full table.

Absolute risk difference belongs in the headline; relative risk belongs in the appendix. A "50% reduction" from 2% to 1% is technically true and practically misleading, and a mixed audience will remember the wrong number. If you use relative risk at all, show it next to the absolute change and, where appropriate, the number needed to treat.

One finding, one chart, one scale

Avoid dual-axis charts, 3D effects, and stacked bars for outcomes — they make comparisons harder precisely when the comparison is the point. Use a simple bar or dot plot with the interval drawn in, keep the y-axis starting at zero for rates, and label the axis with units. If a time-to-event curve is essential, add a short caption explaining what a step means.

Put safety on its own slide, not as a footnote under efficacy. Adverse events, discontinuations and the population they were observed in deserve the same evidence block as the primary endpoint.

Translate without changing the claim

A useful pattern is a two-line stack under each result: the technical statement, then a plain-language restatement directly below it. The restatement must not strengthen the finding — "associated with" stays "associated with", not "caused by". Keep abbreviations in a definitions slide in the appendix and spell them out on first use, even for terms your team says daily.

For topics with regulatory or promotional sensitivity, state the approved indication and the data source on the slide, keep off-label discussion out of the deck unless it is explicitly part of an internal scientific exchange, and have the medical or legal reviewer sign off on the final file. This is presentation structure, not regulatory advice.

The sequence that works

  1. Question and population — who was studied, and what decision this evidence informs.
  2. Study design in one diagram — arms, sample sizes, follow-up.
  3. Primary endpoint — one slide, full evidence block.
  4. Secondary endpoints — one per slide, or a compact table with intervals.
  5. Subgroups — only pre-specified ones, with interaction information available in the appendix.
  6. Safety — its own slide, same structure.
  7. Interpretation — what the finding does and does not support.
  8. Limitations and open questions — stated before the room asks.
  9. Appendix — full tables, definitions, protocol, data cut, references.
